## Description

**Two posters for the wall, printed at the size you actually have.**

Poster A is the ampacity chart — copper and aluminum, 60 °C, 75 °C and 90 °C, twenty sizes from 14 AWG to 1000 kcmil — plus the rule that turns an ampacity into a breaker: the device must not exceed the conductor's ampacity at the termination temperature, and you take the largest standard rating at or below that number. It also prints the three pairings that need no rounding (14 / 12 / 10 AWG copper at 60 °C to 15 / 20 / 30 A), because on those the ampacity is already a standard breaker size.

Poster B is GFCI and AFCI: where each requirement lives in the code (210.8 for GFCI, 210.12 for AFCI), what changed in the 2026 edition — the garage item in 210.8(A)(2), the Class C SPGFCI exception for HVAC equipment in 210.8(F), the "or switch" wording in 210.12(E) — and the safety notes that come before any of it matters: de-energize, verify the absence of voltage, lockout/tagout, qualified and authorized only.

None of that was filled in from memory: a wrong row on a breaker poster is worse than a missing one.

**The gaps are printed, not hidden.** Poster A's small print states that the 60 °C values above 2 AWG, the aluminum 60 °C column and the 400/700 kcmil rows are not shown. A dash in the table means "not rated or not listed", never 0 A. 14 AWG aluminum has no value at any temperature and the poster does not invent one.

**Edition honesty.** The NEC is not law by itself — it applies where a jurisdiction adopts it, usually with amendments and a lag. New York City, for one, enforces the 2020 edition and deletes Type XHWN from the 90 °C columns; Minnesota states it will not enforce 210.8(F) in one replacement scenario. Both posters say, in print, that the edition your AHJ enforces governs and that you check it before you wire.

**Where the numbers come from.** Ampacities are restated from NFPA 70 (2026) Table 310.16 as reproduced by the Cerrowire ampacity chart. The GFCI and AFCI change items are restated from the Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry "2026 NEC FAQ" and the New York City Electrical Code (2025). The posters restate values and paraphrase requirements; they do not reproduce the standard's tables, notes or code text.
