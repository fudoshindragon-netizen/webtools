GFCI & AFCI Basics

Ground-fault and arc-fault protection — where the requirement lives, what changed in 2026, and the safety notes that come first
Edition note. Requirements shown are from the 2026 NEC unless your AHJ has adopted a different edition — check before you wire. GFCI and AFCI rules are the most heavily amended part of the code: the edition in force where you work, and that jurisdiction’s amendments, decide the answer.

GFCIGround-fault circuit-interrupter  ·  NEC 210.8

GFCI protection guards against ground-fault shock. The requirement sits in Section 210.8 of Article 210, and it is a list of locations, not a general rule for every receptacle.

  • 210.8(A) — the list for dwelling units.
  • 210.8(B) — the separate list for other than dwelling units.
  • Further rules cover particular equipment and specific situations.

Which receptacles and locations appear on those lists changes between editions and is amended locally. Read the list in the edition your AHJ enforces — it is not reproduced on this poster.

2026 renumbering: the dwelling list gains a separate item for garages (now item 2), and the words “that have a floor located at or below grade level” are removed from it — elevation does not remove the shock hazard.

Source NFPA 70 (2026) 210.8, via the Minnesota DLI “2026 NEC FAQ”, 14 Nov 2026.

AFCIArc-fault circuit-interrupter  ·  NEC 210.12

AFCI protection guards against arc faults in branch-circuit wiring. The requirement sits in Section 210.12 of Article 210 and applies to the branch circuits listed there.

Principally that means 120-volt, single-phase, 15- and 20-ampere branch circuits supplying outlets or devices in dwelling-unit rooms — but the list, its exceptions and the permitted device types are the code’s, and the code’s list is what governs.

Extensions and replacements are covered too. When an existing branch circuit is extended, modified or replaced, the code says where the AFCI device may go.

2026 change: a listed outlet branch-circuit AFCI (OBCAFCI) may now be installed at the first receptacle outlet or switch of the existing circuit — the words “or switch” are new in 210.12(E).

Source NFPA 70 (2026) 210.12, via the Minnesota DLI “2026 NEC FAQ”, 14 Nov 2026.

What changed in the 2026 editionVerified against the Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry “2026 NEC FAQ”, 14 November 2026

210.8(A)(2) — garages

A new list item separates garages from item (3), which now covers accessory buildings not intended as habitable rooms and limited to storage, work and similar areas. The phrase “that have a floor located at or below grade level” was removed, because elevation does not remove the hazard. A habitable room inside or above a garage is not itself caught by the garage item.

210.8(F) — dwelling outdoor outlets

A new exception permits Class C GFCI (SPGFCI) protection for listed HVAC equipment after 1 September 2026, and where a Class C SPGFCI is provided the disconnect must be marked with the warning “Class C SPGFCI protection is provided for an HVAC unit”. 210.8(F) does not reach outdoor outlets that are not readily accessible — submersible well pumps, sewer lift pumps, load management controllers, surge protective devices and similar — nor outdoor lighting outlets.

210.12(E) — extensions

In list item (2), the words “or switch” were added, so a listed outlet branch-circuit AFCI (OBCAFCI) may be installed at the first receptacle outlet or switch of the existing branch circuit.

Source Minnesota DLI “2026 NEC FAQ” (6 pp.), items 3–5 of its change list, 14 Nov 2026 — a state authority’s account of the edition, not the code itself.

Safety notesBefore anything on this poster matters

  • De-energize, then verify. Open the disconnect, then verify the absence of voltage at the point of work with a tester you have proved works.
  • Follow lockout/tagout. Use your employer’s procedure; a GFCI or AFCI is not a substitute for de-energizing.
  • Qualified and authorized only. Work on electrical equipment only if you are both — a reference poster does not make anyone competent.
  • The manufacturer’s instructions govern the device — test buttons, trip levels, wiring terminals and required markings come from the listing, not from memory.

Source 29 CFR 1910 Subpart S and 1910.147 — framing only; not re-verified for this printing.

Why this poster is not the answer sheetEdition lag and local amendment

The NEC is not law by itself. It applies where a state or local jurisdiction adopts it, usually with amendments and a lag. New York City, for one, enforces the 2020 NEC.

One national answer will be wrong somewhere. Minnesota, in the same FAQ this poster draws on, states that it will not enforce 210.8(F) in one replacement scenario, citing its own statute. Check your AHJ’s amendments before you rely on any item above.

The itemised GFCI and AFCI location lists are deliberately not printed here. They belong to the code you are working under. Read them there.

Sources NYC Electrical Code (2025) § 28-1101.1 for the adoption lag; Minnesota DLI FAQ for its 210.8(F) position. Both 14 Nov 2026.
