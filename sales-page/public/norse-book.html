Excerpt — the opening (Ch. 1)

In the beginning there was no sand, nor sea, nor cool waves, neither earth beneath nor heaven above. There was only the great yawning gulf, which is called Ginnungagap, and it was without form and void, a magical emptiness stretching between that which was not yet. And to the north of the gulf lay Niflheim, the world of mist and of freezing cold. In that dark realm was the well called Hvergelmir, and from it flowed eleven rivers of bitter venom. These rivers ran southward into Ginnungagap, and their waters hardened into ice and poisonous rime that filled the northern part of the void with grinding cold.

Chapter 1 — The Void and the First Beings

The story

In the beginning there was no sand, nor sea, nor cool waves — only the yawning gulf of Ginnungagap, where the ice of Niflheim met the fire of Muspelheim and the first giant, Ymir, drew breath.

This book retells the Norse myths in a deliberately ancient, scriptural voice, from the first melting of the ice to the last battle and beyond. Watch Odin, Vili and Vé slay Ymir and build the Nine Worlds from his body. Meet the Æsir and the Vanir, the Norns at the well of fate, and Loki with his terrible children: the wolf Fenrir, the Midgard Serpent and Hel. Follow the death of bright Baldr, the vengeance of Váli, Thor's journeys against the giants, the Fimbulwinter and the fall of the gods at Ragnarök.

Then the story keeps going where the old songs fall quiet. In the later chapters the green earth rises unsown, Baldr and Höðr sit together in the grass, Thor's sons keep the hammer Mjölnir, and a new generation learns why the tale must be told again every evening: that peace is bought at a price, and that a story kept open can still teach the living.

Fifty short chapters, each a single sitting. For readers of the Eddas, of mythology retellings, and of anyone who likes their old stories told plainly, with weight.

More from the text

He returned to the place where the gods were at their sport. There stood Höðr, the blind brother of Baldr, apart from the others, for he could not see to join the casting. Loki put the mistletoe dart into his hand and said, “Why do you not honor Baldr as the others do? I will guide your arm.” Höðr cast, and the mistletoe flew true and pierced Baldr through. Baldr fell dead upon the ground, and it was the greatest misfortune that had ever befallen gods or men.
