How to study organic chemistry when you have four weeks and no time

OChem punishes re-reading and rewards retrieval. Here is a four-week plan mapped to the order most courses teach in, the twelve reaction families worth over-learning, and the exam-day habit that stops you drawing arrows before you know what the question is.

Version 1.0 · Written 2026-11-14 · Study aid — not a substitute for your course, your textbook or your lecturer

Organic chemistry is the one course where reading harder stops working. A mechanism is not a fact to be recognised on a page; it is a decision you have to make, under time pressure, from a substrate and a set of conditions. Four weeks is enough time to build that decision — but only if most of the four weeks is spent recalling, not re-reading.

Why OChem punishes reading and rewards retrieval

You can read the SN1 mechanism five times, nod at every arrow, and still freeze in an exam when you are shown a tertiary alkyl halide in aqueous ethanol. The page taught you to recognise a drawing. The exam asks you to produce one from a description. Those are different skills, and only the second one is graded.

So the study method follows the exam, not the textbook. Three habits do most of the work:

  • Start from the reagent pair, not the product. Cover the answer, look at the substrate and the conditions, and say out loud which family this is and what the product will have to look like. Then draw it and check.
  • Say the decision rule in one line. "Strong nucleophile, primary carbon, polar aprotic — backside attack, inversion." If you cannot compress a family into one line, you do not have it yet.
  • Do a small number of problems cold, every day. Five mechanisms drawn from memory beats twenty mechanisms read while looking at the answers.

Mechanisms also answer the question the list-memorisers never get: why. Once you know that a carbocation will form only where it is stabilised, you can predict a product you have never seen. That is what the harder marks are for.

A four-week plan, mapped to the usual course order

Most organic chemistry courses run in roughly this order, because each block uses the one before it. Yours may differ — check your syllabus and move the weeks to match it. What should not move is the shape: two weeks on the fundamentals and the reaction families, one on carbonyls and aromatic chemistry, one on spectroscopy and mixed retrieval.

Two things about this plan that matter more than the table. Week 4 is not revision of week 1 — it is the week where you find out what you cannot recall, and the point of the mixed sets is to expose gaps early enough to fix them. And if your course runs spectroscopy early, do spectroscopy early: it is the topic that gets easier with repetition, so it wants the longest run-up you can give it.

The twelve reaction families worth over-learning

Courses differ, so treat this as our list rather than a syllabus — check it against your own learning outcomes and add whatever your course adds. The twelve are the families that do the heaviest lifting in the first-year sequences we have looked at, not a count of exam marks. For each one, learn the tell (what in the question points at this family) as well as the mechanism.

  1. Radical halogenation of alkanes. CH4 + Cl2 with light or heat gives a mixture of chlorinated products. Tell: an alkane, a halogen, light or heat, no solvent doing any work. Know why it is unselective and why bromination is more selective.
  2. SN2. One step, backside attack, inversion of configuration, rate depends on both partners. Tell: methyl or primary carbon, a good nucleophile, a polar aprotic solvent.
  3. SN1. Two steps through a carbocation, racemisation at the reacting centre, rearrangement possible. Tell: tertiary or benzylic/allylic carbon, a weak nucleophile, a polar protic solvent, heat not required.
  4. E2. One step, anti-periplanar, needs a strong base. Tell: strong base, secondary or tertiary substrate, heat. Know that a bulky base such as tert-butoxide gives the less substituted alkene, and be able to say why.
  5. E1. Carbocation first, then loss of a proton, Zaitsev-favoured. Tell: weak base, polar protic, heat, tertiary substrate — and usually SN1 products alongside.
  6. Electrophilic addition to alkenes. Markovnikov with HX; anti-Markovnikov with HBr and peroxides; syn addition with H2 and a metal catalyst; halohydrin formation in water. Tell: a C=C and an electrophile, no strong base anywhere.
  7. Hydroboration–oxidation. Anti-Markovnikov, syn, and no free carbocation — which is why it works where a carbocation would rearrange. Tell: borane or a dialkylborane, then H2O2/hydroxide in the second step.
  8. Epoxidation and ring opening. A peracid gives the epoxide; acid-catalysed opening attacks the more substituted carbon, base-catalysed opening attacks the less substituted one. Tell: a peracid, then water with acid or with base — the conditions decide the regio chemistry.
  9. Electrophilic aromatic substitution. Nitration, halogenation, sulfonation, Friedel–Crafts alkylation and acylation. Tell: an aromatic ring plus a strong electrophile and usually a Lewis acid. Learn the directing rules: activators send ortho/para, deactivators (except halogens) send meta. Know that Friedel–Crafts alkylation can rearrange and does not work on strongly deactivated rings.
  10. Nucleophilic addition to carbonyls. Grignard or organolithium reagents and hydride sources give alcohols after aqueous workup. NaBH4 handles aldehydes and ketones; LiAlH4 is strong enough for esters, carboxylic acids and amides. Tell: C=O plus a nucleophile that cannot leave — so the addition sticks.
  11. Acetal and ketal formation. A carbonyl plus two alcohols under acid catalysis, reversible, and a diol gives a ring. Tell: acid, an alcohol, and a question about protecting a ketone while something else reacts.
  12. Acyl substitution. Acid chlorides to esters and amides; Fischer esterification under acid catalysis at equilibrium; ester and amide hydrolysis. Tell: C=O with a leaving group attached — the nucleophile replaces the leaving group instead of adding and stopping.

If your syllabus also lists the aldol and Claisen condensations, treat them as a thirteenth: they are the α-carbon pair, and they are built out of families 10 and 12, which is why they come last in most courses.

How to test yourself on each, without a tutor

One family at a time, on blank paper, with the notes shut:

  1. Write the family name at the top and the tell underneath it, in your own words, in one line.
  2. Give yourself a substrate and a set of conditions from your notes, then draw the mechanism from memory: every arrow, every charge, every stereochemical consequence.
  3. Open the notes. Mark it. If an arrow was in the wrong place, write the reason beside it — not the correct arrow, the reason.
  4. Do the same family again tomorrow, cold. If you get it right twice from memory, it moves to a weekly review.

The test is not "can I follow this when it is in front of me". It is "can I produce it when it is not".

Flashcards done properly

Most OChem flashcards fail for the same reason: too much on the back. A card with a paragraph on it becomes a card you read instead of a card you answer. The rules that make cards work:

  • One prompt per card. One substrate, one set of conditions, one question: which family, and what is the product's defining feature?
  • No paragraphs on the back. A name, a one-line rule, or a single small drawing. If the back needs a paragraph, split the card in two.
  • Answer in ten seconds or it is two cards. A card you cannot answer quickly is testing several things at once, which means it is teaching you none of them.
  • Keep the arrows off the prompt. The arrows are the answer. A card that shows the mechanism and asks you to name it is a recognition drill, not a retrieval drill — useful once, then useless.
  • Review on a schedule, not when you feel like it: the day you make the card, then a few days later, then a week, then a fortnight. Cards you keep getting right can wait longer; cards you keep missing should come back tomorrow.

Phone drilling works well for the one-line rules and the reagent pairs, because those are exactly the things you can do in a queue. Mechanism drawing needs paper and a pencil — it is a motor skill as much as a memory one, and drawing it is the practice.

Exam-day strategy: name the class before you write anything

Lost marks in OChem are often not lost on chemistry at all. They are lost by starting to draw before deciding what kind of question it is. The habit that fixes this costs about twenty seconds per question:

  1. Read the whole question first. Substrate, reagent, conditions, solvent, heat, light, acid or base. Those words are the question; the structure is only half of it.
  2. Write the family name in the margin. "E2." "Acetal formation." "Friedel–Crafts acylation." If you can name it, the mechanism usually follows; if you cannot, you have found the real problem.
  3. Find the nucleophile and the electrophile, then the leaving group, then the stereochemistry. In that order. This is also what to do when the family name will not come.
  4. Only then draw arrows. Arrows drawn before the class is named are the single most common way to produce a mechanism that is internally tidy and chemically wrong.
  5. If you are stuck, write what you do know. The intermediate, the polarities, the reason one product is favoured. Marking schemes award steps, and a correct intermediate with a wrong ending scores better than a blank.

Then check the two things examiners look for and students forget: charges balance, and stereochemistry is stated where the question implies it. A mechanism with a missing formal charge reads as a mechanism you did not understand.
