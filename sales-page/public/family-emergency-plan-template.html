How to write a family emergency plan that survives contact with a real emergency

A plan is not a page of good intentions. It answers five questions, it lives on paper, and it gets practised twice a year. Here is the template, plus a worked example for the household big plans usually ignore: two adults, two children and an elderly parent who cannot move fast or far.

Version 1.0 · Written 2026-11-14 · Plan content checked against Ready.gov (FEMA) guidance, accessed 2026-11-14 · Written for households in the United States

A family emergency plan is not a page of good intentions. It is a short set of answers that still work when the power is out, the phones are jammed and the person who usually handles everything is on the other side of the city. This page gives you the template, then fills it in for the household the big plans usually ignore: two adults, two children, and an elderly parent who cannot move fast or far.

Where the guidance comes from. What a household plan should contain is set by Ready.gov's Make a Plan guidance (FEMA), which covers how you receive alerts, a shelter plan, an evacuation route, a communication plan and the household-specific needs of each member — plus FEMA's fillable Family Emergency Communication Plan card. Everything below is organised around that, in the order a household actually has to decide things. Checked 2026-11-14. Your local emergency management office's instructions always win over this page.

The five questions a plan has to answer

Every family plan that has ever been used in a real emergency came down to five questions. If your plan answers them, in writing, and everyone in the household has read the answers, you have a plan. If it answers ten other things but not these five, you have a document.

1. Where do we meet?

Two places, not one. Place A is close — the lobby, the stairwell landing, the tree on the corner — the place you go when you have to leave the flat in a hurry and the building is still standing. Place B is outside the neighbourhood, reachable on foot, and is where you go if you cannot get back into the building at all. A second place outside the area matters most for the household member who is out when it happens.

Write both down with enough detail that a child could find them: not "the library" but "the library on the corner of the second street past the pharmacy, the one with the blue doors".

2. Who do we call?

One person outside the region — the out-of-area contact. It is the single most useful line on the sheet, and it gets its own section below.

3. Who collects which child?

Name one adult per child, and name a backup for each. Then deal with the part that fails most often: the school or nursery release rule. Most schools will not release a child to someone who is not on the authorised list, no matter how sensible the reason. If a neighbour is your backup, that neighbour has to be on the list before the day you need them, and the school needs a current phone number for them.

Write the answer as a sentence, because that is how it will be used: "If neither parent can reach the school by 16:00, [neighbour, flat 3] collects [child] and takes them to Place A."

4. Where are the documents?

Identification, insurance, medication lists, a copy of the tenancy or deed, and the children's health records. Decide three things: what is in the folder, where the folder lives (one bag, one place, everyone knows where), and who carries it if the household leaves separately. Paper, in a plastic sleeve. The originals stay where they always are; the folder holds copies, because a folder you are afraid to lose is a folder you will not take.

5. What do we do if the phones are down?

Decide the fallback in advance: two check-in times (a morning and an evening), the place each person walks to if nobody answers, and the rule that a short text goes out before anyone tries to call. Ready.gov's guidance is to text rather than call where possible, because a short message can get through a network that has no room for a voice call. Add the paper copy: the numbers you would need, printed, in every bag and on the fridge, because the contact list that lives inside the phone is the contact list you cannot open when the phone is dead.

The template itself — five lines

This is the whole thing at minimum. Copy it onto a sheet of paper, fill it in by hand, and put a copy in every bag. It is free to use and print; nothing here is held back for a paid product.

Place A (in or at the building): ____________________________________

Place B (outside the neighbourhood, on foot): _________________________

Out-of-area contact — name, number, town: _____________________________

Child collection — child → adult → backup → school release note on file?: ____________________________________________

Documents — what is in the folder, where it lives, who carries it: ______________________________

Phones down — check-in times (a.m. / p.m.) and where each person walks: ______________________________

Why a plan that lives on a phone fails in a blackout

Four failures, and they happen together:

  • The battery is the first casualty. The phone is the thing you were going to charge from the thing that stopped working. By the second evening a plan that exists only on a screen exists only in the memory of whoever read it last.
  • The network jams in exactly the situation the plan is for. Everyone in the area picks up their phone at once. A voice call is the first thing to fail; a short text is more likely to get through, which is why the plan says text first.
  • The list is behind the lock screen. The elderly parent at the other address does not have your contact list, your neighbour's number or the school's number — and cannot get them from a phone that is flat, locked, or in someone else's pocket.
  • Nobody can read it together. A plan has to be read out loud, at a kitchen table, by the people it covers. That is how you find the gaps. You cannot do that with five phones.

So print it. One copy in each bag, one on the fridge, one in the car, one at the elderly parent's flat, and one in the folder with the documents. Plastic sleeve, not a drawer — a drawer is where plans go to be forgotten.

A worked example: two adults, two children, one elderly parent

The household the flagship program is built around. Five people, one of whom is 76 and walks with a frame, one of whom is four. Here is what the five lines look like filled in, and the hard limits each person brings to them.

Adult ACollects Child 1 from school; carries the document folderFolder, one page plan, charged phone, spare batteryWorks 12 miles away; cannot leave before the shift ends
Adult BStays with the elderly parent; runs Place AOne page plan, medication list, keys to the parent's flatCannot drive if the second car is blocked in the garage

Elderly parent (76)Stays put; calls the out-of-area contact; opens the door to Adult B onlyOne page plan, a week of medication, reading glassesOne flight of stairs at most; no lift means no leaving the flat

Then the numbers that turn the table into something real. Measure them once, write them down:

  • How long Adult A's walk home actually takes — walk it, time it, and add the time it takes to get out of the building. If it is 70 minutes, then "collects Child 1 by 16:00" is only true on a day the shift ends early.
  • How many flights, and whether the lift needs power — for the parent, and for getting a frame down a stairwell in the dark.
  • Where the medication list is, on paper, in the parent's flat and in the folder. Not a diagnosis, not a history — the drugs, the doses, and the prescriber's number.
  • Who the parent will actually open the door to, and how that person proves it is them. Write the answer down; in a blackout the intercom does not work.

Notice what the example does not contain: a plan to move a 76-year-old down four flights in the dark. That is not a plan, it is a hope. The household's plan has to be built on the parent staying put, with the supplies and the contact sheet that make staying put survivable, and with one named adult who can get to them.

The out-of-area contact convention

One person, outside your region, who knows in advance that they are the contact. Everyone in the household checks in with that person — not with each other, not in a group chat that may or may not load. The contact then tells the others what they have heard.

Why outside the area: local circuits are the ones that jam, and a call or text to another region is often the one that gets through. Why one person: because five people calling five people produces a mess of half-information, and because a child can remember one number.

  • Ask them first. A contact who did not agree to be the contact will not be watching their phone.
  • Give them a paper list of every household member's number, the school's, the nursery's and the neighbour's. Not a screenshot.
  • Agree the wording. Two short lines is enough: who is safe, where they are, what they need. Agree a phrase that means "I am fine, no reply needed" so the contact is not left waiting.
  • Agree the times. The same two check-in times as the phones-down line, so nobody is guessing.
  • Refresh it when a number changes. A contact with last year's numbers is worse than no contact, because everyone assumes it is handled.

What to review, and when

Twice a year, on two dates you pick now and put in a calendar — many households use the two clock-change weekends because they already happen twice a year and nobody has to remember them. And once more after any real use, whether that was a two-hour outage or something worse.

The review is twenty minutes and it has four steps:

  1. Read the five lines out loud to each person in the household, including the children, in the words on the page.
  2. Ask each person to say back the two places and the out-of-area number. If the nine-year-old cannot, the plan is not finished — that is the whole point of writing it down.
  3. Correct anything that changed: a phone number, a school, a job, a medication, someone moving in or out, a neighbour who moved away.
  4. Reprint and replace every copy, including the ones in the bags and at the parent's flat. A plan that says "corrected" in one place and last year's version in another is worse than one plan.

After a real use, add a fifth step: write down what failed. Not what went well — what failed. The call that did not go through, the number that was wrong, the child who did not know where to go, the folder that was not where it was supposed to be. Those notes are the next version of the plan.

What this page will not tell you

No statistics. You will find plenty of pages claiming a percentage of households are unprepared, or that a certain number of families have no plan. We have no source for any of those figures and we will not invent one. The argument for writing a plan does not need a statistic: it is twenty minutes of work that you do once, and the alternative is improvising it in the dark.

No advice on whether to stay or go. That is your local authority's call, made on the day, and it overrides anything you have written down. A plan is how your household carries out that instruction quickly, not a substitute for it.

No medical, legal or tactical advice. The medication line is a list of what to carry, not a recommendation. Weapons use, weapons handling and weapons construction are out of scope of everything we publish.
